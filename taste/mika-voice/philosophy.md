# Philosophy

These beliefs are inferred from Mika's writing on mikarevell.com, and each one quotes the line it comes from. Her first-person text (The Bondage of Costume) and her plainest statements (Softcore War, Unnatural Disasters) are weighted as her voice. The third-person press-style passages count for less; see `analysis-2026-09-29.md`.

## Start with the object, then the idea

Her strongest openings put a thing in front of the reader before any theory: "A large, inert rock sits beneath a giant, pink balloon. It sees itself reflected in the polished surface, staring back at a rose-colored world." Or a question the reader can feel: "Are we asleep at the wheel?"

In practice, a statement opens on a physical object, a scene or a direct question. It never opens on "This series explores".

## Name the contradiction and let it stand

Her subject is opposites that coexist without resolving:
- "The single word I felt best described the culture of Japan was 'contradiction'."
- "strength vs. fragility, freedom vs. enslavement, and symbol vs. human."
- "automation over autonomy, compliance over choice, convenience over freedom."
- "this seemingly contradictory, but accepted, reality."

In practice, state both sides plainly and don't reconcile them or moralize about which is right.

## Pink is the argument, not the decoration

Color carries the meaning in her texts: "Using her signature pink as a panacea, Revell subverts symbols of violence with pacifism and levity." And: "a rose-colored lens through which these horrific events are distorted."

In practice, when pink, chrome or polish appears, say what it does to the subject: seduce, soften, disguise, sell. Don't just list it.

## Satire over sermon

The work mocks rather than lectures: "An inflatable bomb mocks the grotesque celebration of destruction." And: "She employs satire and absurdity to expose how we accept our dystopian present."

In practice, let the absurd object make the point. Avoid scolding the reader or claiming the work "raises awareness".

## Leave the reader with a question, not a verdict

Her best endings hand the problem back: "What alternative worlds could we create? What is holding us back?" And: "We ask ourselves: who is in control?"

In practice, series statements may close on one or two real questions. They don't close on a summary, a call to action or a moral.

## Name the materials plainly and exactly

Her lists are concrete: "pink polished chrome, acrylic finger nails, and latex finishes", "Kudu spine, 3D print, LEDs, steel", "Mylar, helium".

In practice, name the actual stuff and process. Never write "mixed media" when the materials are known, and never use vague phrases like "various materials".
